Say This 01
01
The
ice-breaker
line
"Your kid's in Mrs. Chen's class, right?"

One shared detail is all it takes. A teacher's name, the same school bus, a jacket you recognize — it opens the door without making anyone feel put on the spot.

💡
You don't need a clever opener. Shared context is the whole trick. "I think our kids know each other" works every time.

Say This 02
02
The
soft ask
that lands
"We should do a park afternoon sometime."

Low-pressure, no date attached. It signals you're open to more without putting them on the spot. Most people will say yes — and mean it — because there's nothing to commit to yet.

💡
The key word is "sometime." It takes the pressure off both of you and turns a casual hello into an open invitation.

Say This 03
03
Ask for their
local intel
"Do you know the best playground around here?"

People love sharing what they know about their neighbourhood. A simple "where do you take the kids on weekends?" gives them something easy to answer and makes the conversation feel natural.

💡
Asking for recommendations is a compliment. It says: "You know this place better than I do." Everyone likes being that person.

5 / 7
Say This 04
04
Give before
you ask
"We're doing a park run Saturday — you're welcome to join."

The fastest way to make a parent friend is to offer first. Invite them to something you're already doing. It removes the awkwardness of a formal "playdate request" — and makes you the connector.

💡
You're not asking them to commit. You're just leaving the door open. "No pressure" means it — and that's what makes people say yes.

Say This 05
05
Follow up
within
48 hours
"Hey! It was great chatting — still keen for that park day?"

Most parent friendships die between "we should do something" and never doing it. The window is small — follow up while it's still fresh and the warmth from your first chat is still there.

💡
After 48 hours it starts to feel weird. Before 48 hours it feels natural. Set a reminder if you have to — future you will thank you.
